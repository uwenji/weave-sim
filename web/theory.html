From rod to weave

How WeaveSim turns timber laths, their crossings and the friction between them into equations a computer can relax — the rod model, the geometry of a crossing, the solver, and the safeguards that keep it honest.

WeaveSim is the simulation tool of the Kagome Architectures project at the Royal Danish Academy (Chair for Biohybrid Architecture), developed by You-Wen Ji. This page describes the solver in web/js/solver_cpu.js and web/js/rod_cosserat.js, and its WebGPU twin, as of 20 October 2026.

1One iteration in one picture

WeaveSim never solves a woven shell in one go. It repeats one small cycle thousands of times: look at where the laths are; at every crossing, work out where they touch, how hard they press and how much they rub; turn all of that into small pushes and twists on the laths; move everything a little; and start again — until nothing moves any more.

In the code, one pass of the iteration loop in CpuSolver.step(n) is one turn of this loop (the app calls it ten iterations at a time). CosseratRod.apply (rod_cosserat.js) adds the rod forces and torques (§3–§5); PolylineContact.apply evaluates both laths at every crossing (§6), applies the contact, friction and seat laws (§8–§10) and distributes the result back to nodes and segment frames (§7); the integrator (§11) moves the nodes, turns the frames about their three axes and applies kinetic damping. The safeguards of §12–§13 sit inside these steps, not around them.

2The weave comes first

A kagome weave is the pattern of a triaxial basket: three families of strips, each pair crossing at about 60°, leaving hexagons and triangles between them. WeaveSim draws it on any triangulated surface. Every edge of the mesh becomes one crossing, every triangle contributes three short pieces of lath, and the pieces join into long strips. Which strip lies on top at each crossing is settled before any physics, by a rule that makes every strip go over, under, over, under.

The kagome is the medial graph of the triangulation: a knot at the midpoint of every mesh edge and a small triangle inside every face. A strand entering a face across edge $(p,q)$, having pivoted around $q$, leaves across the edge that contains $p$ and pivots around $p$ next; the pivots alternate, so strands are straight on a regular lattice. Open strands start at boundary edges; on a closed mesh they close into loops. A mesh with $F$ faces and $E$ edges gives $3F$ lath segments and $E$ knots, each knot shared by exactly two strands.

The second condition makes both strands agree that exactly one of them is on top. It is a 2-colouring of the strand graph, solved by breadth-first search; cycles whose required parities sum to an odd number (irregular valences, some boundaries) are reported as conflicts and left to the designer, and flip knots invert single crossings for skip weaves. A greedy assignment was tried first in Grasshopper and failed near boundaries — the BFS replaced it in WeaveInit v3.

The computer then lifts each strip half a lath thickness where it passes over, and lowers it where it passes under, so that the very first picture already has the laths resting on each other.

Between two knots the centreline is a centripetal Catmull–Rom curve $\mathbf c(t)$ through the on-surface knot points, offset along the interpolated normal with a cosine blend of the two knot offsets $o_k=\pm h/2$:

Section frames ride on every node: $\mathbf Z$ along the tangent, $\mathbf Y$ the surface normal (the lath's thickness direction) and $\mathbf X=\mathbf Y\times\mathbf Z$ across the width. At the knots, $\mathbf Y$ is taken from the crossing itself, $\mathbf t_A\times\mathbf t_B$, not from the facet normal of the mesh, and interpolated between knots. With facet normals the frame and the crossing disagreed by the dihedral error (3–5° on coarse meshes), which the section-aware gap of §8 turned into millimetres of false penetration and kilonewtons of force at the first iteration.

A weave also has a smallest size. Between an under and an over crossing a lath changes level by one thickness $h$; with the flats where it sits on the crossing laths, the free length must satisfy $L_{f,\min} = 2\sqrt{\rho_{\min} h} = h\sqrt{2/\varepsilon_{\max}}$. For 60 × 30 mm hardwood at $\varepsilon_{\max}=0.4\,\%$ that is a knot spacing of about 740 mm (mesh edges of about 1.5 m); for compressed wood it depends on the strain limit still to be measured. The solver does not enforce this — too fine a mesh simply shows up as very large moments.

3A lath is a rod with a frame

To a computer a lath is a line in space — its centreline — with a little set of arrows riding along it: one across the width, one through the thickness, one along the lath. Bending tilts the arrows, twisting spins them about the lath. A 60 × 30 mm lath bends easily the flat way and four times less easily on edge, like a ruler; twisting is cheaper still. A rod that carries such a frame is called a Cosserat rod.

A Cosserat rod is a centreline $\mathbf r(s)$ and a director frame $R(s) = [\,\cX{\mathbf X}\;\cY{\mathbf Y}\;\cZ{\mathbf Z}\,]$ that may rotate independently of it. Its strains are the stretch and shear of the centreline seen in the frame, and the rate at which the frame turns:

For the 60 × 30 mm lath with $E = 10\,000$ N/mm² and $G = 625$ N/mm²: $I_x = wh^3/12 = 1.35\times10^5$ mm⁴ (flatwise: bending about the width axis X, deflection along Y), $I_y = hw^3/12 = 5.4\times10^5$ mm⁴ (edgewise, four times stiffer), and Roark's rectangle constant $J = ab^3\big[\tfrac13 - 0.21\tfrac{b}{a}\big(1-\tfrac{b^4}{12a^4}\big)\big] \approx 3.7\times10^5$ mm⁴. Hence $EI_x = 1.35\times10^9$, $EI_y = 5.4\times10^9$ and $GJ \approx 2.3\times10^8$ N·mm².

The term $\mathbf r'\times\mathbf n$ in the moment balance is worth remembering: it is where the shape of the bent rod enters its own equilibrium, and §5 will show that it is the whole difference between first- and second-order beam theory.

4The discrete rod: every piece its own frame

The computer cuts the smooth lath into short straight pieces, four between two crossings unless told otherwise. Every piece carries its own set of arrows, and the arrows are free to turn: about the lath, and also a little against it. Along each piece two springs act: one resists stretching it, the other resists tipping its arrows against it — that tipping is shear. Where two pieces meet, the computer measures how far the arrows of one are turned against those of the other, as one true angle about one axis: turned about the width, the lath bends flatwise; about the thickness, edgewise; about the lath itself, it twists. Stretch, shear, bending and twist all come from one stored energy, and every push and twist the rod hands out is that energy running downhill.

Each segment $j$, from node $a$ to node $b$ with rest length $l_j^0$, carries a full rotation $\mathbf R_j$ (a unit quaternion $q_j$) that is not tied to its chord: a discrete Cosserat rod of the Simo–Reissner kind. Its strain is the chord seen in its own frame — the discrete $\boldsymbol\Gamma$ of eq. — and its energy the stretch and shear terms of eq. , with the shear area $A_s = \kappa_sA$ of a solid rectangle, $\kappa_s = 5/6$:

$\sigma_1, \sigma_2$ are the shear strains along the width and the normal, $\sigma_3$ the stretch. The segment force $\mathbf n_j = \mathbf R_j\,\mathrm{diag}(\kappa_sGA,\ \kappa_sGA,\ EA)\,\boldsymbol\sigma_j$ acts as $+\mathbf n_j$ on node $a$ and $-\mathbf n_j$ on node $b$, and as the torque $(\mathbf x_b-\mathbf x_a)\times\mathbf n_j$ on the segment's frame: the discrete $\mathbf r'\times\mathbf n$ of eq. . $\Psi_1, \Psi_2$ are the flatwise and edgewise bending angles, $\Psi_3$ the twist. The length of $\boldsymbol\Psi_i$ is the true angle between the two frames, $\theta = 2\operatorname{atan2}(|\mathbf v|, w)$ for the relative quaternion $(w, \mathbf v) = q_a^{*}\otimes q_b$ taken with $w \ge 0$, so the measure grows steadily all the way to 180°. The bracket is $J_l^{-\mathsf T}(\boldsymbol\Psi)\,\mathbf m$, with $J_l$ the left Jacobian of the rotation group: it turns the moment conjugate to $\boldsymbol\Psi$ into the torque that turns the frames, exactly at any angle ($\beta \to 1/12$ as $\theta \to 0$). For a section with $EI_x \ne EI_y$, $\mathbf m$ is not parallel to $\boldsymbol\Psi$, and $\tfrac12\boldsymbol\Psi\times\mathbf m$ is the roll moment of anisotropic bending: a lath bent off its principal axes is turned about its own axis, so a lath bent about its strong axis can roll sideways and buckle — lateral–torsional buckling. Rotation anchors (rX, rY, rZ) are clamp joints: a joint between the segment's frame and a copy of it frozen when the goals are built, with half a segment of Voronoi length and only the stiffness of the anchored axes (rX → $EI_x$, rY → $EI_y$, rZ → $GJ$).

A woven lath can be stored in two very different ways. Bent cold, it wants to spring back straight, and all the curvature of the weave is stored energy pressing the laths together. Steamed and dried in place, it has taken the woven shape as its own and stores nothing. WeaveSim has one dial between the two, the activation.

At $a = 1$ the lath's zero-energy state is straight and untwisted, as in SOFiSTiK's active-bending (ACTB) approach, so the whole woven curvature is prestress. At $a = 0$ the as-built geometry is the rest state, as for a steam-set lath. In between, a share $a$ of the woven curvature is stored. $\boldsymbol\Omega_{\text{as-built}}$ is the joint rotation $\boldsymbol\Psi_i/\bar l_i$ of the reference frames $q_j^0$, the frames of the woven start (CosseratRod.asBuilt); those frames sit on their chords, so the start carries no shear either. The same parameter carries creep: a relaxation ratio $R(t)$ measured on the material enters as $a(t) = a_0R(t)$, a sequence of equilibria rather than a time integrator.

What shear and roll add

Tipping a section against its lath is shear: the lath deflects a little more than bending alone predicts, most in short, deep spans. And because the turn of a section enters the bending energy, a lath bent about its strong axis can roll sideways and buckle — lateral–torsional buckling. A rod that keeps every section square to its lath and lets it only spin about it, as WeaveSim's earlier rod did, misses both.

For a span between two crossings that sways (or carries a load at its middle), the shear deflection is $12EI/(\kappa_sGAL^2) = 1.2\,(E/G)(d/L)^2$ of the bending deflection for a rectangle with $\kappa_s = 5/6$: about 11 % flatwise and 43 % edgewise for 60 × 30 mm at 400 mm between crossings, 1.5 % and 9 % for the 20 × 8 mm laths at 290 mm. The discrete rod carries this itself — no effective stiffness to correct by hand — and at little cost in speed: for timber $\kappa_sG/E = 0.052$, so $\kappa_sGA$ is about 5 % of $EA$ and the axial spring still sets the masses (§11).

The shear-free rod is the limit $\kappa_sGA \to \infty$: $\gamma_1 = \gamma_2 = 0$, and the frame's third axis is the tangent, $\mathbf Z = \mathbf r'/|\mathbf r'|$. That is the Kirchhoff rod, and WeaveSim's earlier rod was of this kind (frames slaved to their chords, bending from the curvature binormal of Bergou et al. 2008). The discrete rod recovers it: in the advanced beam mode with beam.stiffness_shear = 10⁴, the short cantilever below lands within 0.12 % of the shear-free deflection — a check rather than a setting, since so stiff a shear spring then sets the masses and the relaxation crawls. 5Second-order theory: equilibrium on the bent shape

Engineers distinguish first-order analysis, where forces are worked out on the straight, undeformed lath, from second-order analysis, where they are worked out on the bent one. The difference shows as soon as a lath carries a force along its length: pushing on the ends of a bent lath makes it bend further, pulling straightens it. In a woven shell every lath is already bent and pressed, so these effects are everywhere. WeaveSim never straightens the problem out: it measures every force on the current, bent geometry, which contains second-order theory and goes beyond it to large rotations.

Start from the rod's balance, eq. , with no distributed couple:

$$\mathbf n' + \mathbf f = \mathbf 0, \qquad \mathbf m' + \htmlClass{cQ}{\mathbf r'\times\mathbf n} = \mathbf 0$$

Take a lath in a plane, with deflection $w(x)$ and small rotations $w'\ll1$: tangent, internal force (axial $N$, shear $V$), moment and curvature become

$$\mathbf r' \approx (1,\ w'),\qquad \mathbf n = (N,\ V),\qquad \mathbf m = M\,\mathbf e_z,\qquad \kappa \approx w''$$

Write the balance in components. The cross product contributes $(\mathbf r'\times\mathbf n)_z = V - w'N$:

$$N' = 0,\qquad V' + q = 0,\qquad M' + V \htmlClass{cQ}{\;-\;N\,w'} = 0$$

With $M = EI\,w''$, differentiate the last equation and use the other two. This is the beam-column equation of second-order theory:

$$EI\,w'''' \htmlClass{cQ}{\;-\;N\,w''} = q$$

First-order theory writes equilibrium on the straight axis, which drops the term $N\,w'$ — and with it any effect of the axial force on bending:

$$EI\,w'''' = q$$

In compression $N=-P$ the bracketed stiffness falls and vanishes at Euler's load; for a pinned lath under a load of sine shape the second-order deflection is exactly the first-order one, amplified:

The discrete rod has no separate geometric-stiffness term to forget. The segment force $\mathbf n_j$ of §4 is formed from the current chord seen in the current frame, and the joint torques from the current relative rotations. The moment balance $\mathbf m' + \mathbf r'\times\mathbf n = \mathbf 0$ is the balance of each segment frame: the torques of its two joints against the torque $(\mathbf x_b-\mathbf x_a)\times\mathbf n_j$ of its own shear and stretch spring, on the current chord. So $\mathbf r'\times\mathbf n$ is evaluated on the current geometry at every iteration, with nothing left out. This is also why a bending-active arch in WeaveSim can snap through under load (try it in §11): a first-order model cannot.

6The crossing: two bookmarks that slide

At every crossing the computer keeps two bookmarks: one on lath A and one on lath B, marking the two points of timber that touch. A bookmark is a single number $u$: “3.4” means 40 % of the way along the fourth piece of lath. When the laths slide over each other, the bookmarks move — across the joints between pieces too — so a lath glides over its neighbour instead of catching on the corners of its own polygon.

Each side of each crossing carries a global parameter on its strand's polyline of $N$ segments, decomposed into a segment and a local coordinate. Passing an integer hands the point to the next segment; nothing else needs to happen.

The point itself is evaluated on a cubic Hermite curve through the segment's two nodes, using node tangents $\mathbf T_i$ (the normalised sum of the two adjacent unit chords) and the chord length $L$:

with $h_{00} = 2t^3-3t^2+1$, $h_{10} = t^3-2t^2+t$, $h_{01} = -2t^3+3t^2$, $h_{11} = t^3-t^2$. The curve passes through the nodes with the node tangents, so position and tangent direction are continuous where a bookmark crosses a node: the curve is tangent-continuous ($G^1$; $d\mathbf P/du$ itself changes length with $L$ from one segment to the next).

Where the bookmark sits, the computer also needs to know how the lath's cross-section is turned. It blends the frames of the two nearest nodes, so the section at the crossing is influenced by its neighbours on both sides.

The section frame at $u$ is the normalised linear blend (nlerp) of the two node quaternions — each node quaternion itself being the sign-aligned average of the two adjacent segment frames — re-orthogonalised to the local tangent:

Before the blend, $q_{i+1}$ is flipped to $-q_{i+1}$ if $q_i\cdot q_{i+1} \lt 0$ (both describe the same rotation), so the interpolation takes the short way. So three segment frames ($i-1$, $i$, $i+1$ around the contact segment) shape the section at a crossing: this is the “influence of the neighbouring frames” the contact laws see. Each crossing keeps its own pair of bookmarks rather than searching the whole lath for the closest point, which keeps crossing identity: where two laths cross twice, or a lath crosses itself, a global closest-point search can jump to the wrong crossing in mid-run. The idea — a search area per contact pair, and bookmarks along a lath that never change order — was part of the original design notes for PolylineContact in September.

  • $u$ is clamped to $[0, N]$; a contact never leaves its strand. On the GPU, $u$ is stored as 64-bit fixed point (segment index | 32-bit fraction), so slips add exactly.
  • Node tangents and node frames are recomputed once per iteration for all crossings (_prepare), then both sides are evaluated (_eval(st, 0), _eval(st, 1)).

7Reverse kinematics: handing the push back

The push between two laths is worked out at the bookmark, but the computer can only move nodes and turn frames. So the push is handed back to the two nodes on either side of the bookmark, in proportion to how close it is — like two people carrying a plank: whoever stands nearer to the load carries more of it. And because the push acts at the surface of the lath, not on its centreline, it also tries to turn the lath, mostly rolling it about its own length. That turn goes to the frame of the piece of lath that carries the bookmark.

Forward, the contact point and its frame are functions of the neighbouring nodes and frames (eqs. and ). Reverse, a force $\mathbf F$ acting at the section's support point $\mathbf P + \mathbf r$ (§8) does virtual work on those degrees of freedom; reading the work off term by term gives the generalised forces — the transpose of the forward map:

The torque goes to the frame of segment $j$ whole. A segment frame is a rotation in its own right, free about all three of its axes (§4), so the lever term of eq. needs no conversion: the solver takes the torque into the frame's own axes, $\mathbf R_j^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol\tau_j$, and turns the frame (§11). Its part about the lath rolls the section. Its part across the lath turns the frame against its chord; the shear spring that ties frame and chord answers with the forces $\pm\mathbf n_j$ on the two nodes, and the joints to the neighbouring frames answer with bending moments — so the lath bends. Nothing is split off by hand: at equilibrium the torques on every frame balance,

with the joint torques of eq. (and the scissor torque of §9 where it is on). In the picture: a section rolled onto its edge gets a righting roll from the normal force; friction across the lath rolls it too (the lever $h/2$), while friction along the lath turns the frame about an axis across the lath, and so bends it.

A worked example, from the September design notes. A contact sits between knot 1 and knot 2, at distances 2 and 4 from them, so $t = 1/3$. The shares are $(1-t, t) = (2/3, 1/3)$: the nearer knot takes more — a 4 : 2 split, not 2 : 4 as first written. Getting it backwards produces a subtle instability: sliding towards a knot would reduce that knot's response.

The same weights answer a second question: how much of the contact point's motion can the free nodes actually produce? For the linear part, $\delta\mathbf P = (1-t)\delta\mathbf x_i + t\,\delta\mathbf x_{i+1}$, and a node fixed by an anchor contributes nothing:

The same nodes also carry the crossing's stiffness for the solver: $k_n + k_t$ is added to the fictitious masses of both nodes of the contact segment, and the seat's righting stiffness $k_\theta$ to the rotational inertia of the segment's frame, about all three of its axes (§11).

8Contact: how far apart two laths must be

Two laths cannot occupy the same space. Lying flat on each other, their centrelines are 15 + 15 = 30 mm apart. If one rolls onto its edge it becomes “taller” in the direction of the other — its half-height grows from 15 mm to as much as 33.5 mm when a corner points straight at it, putting the centrelines 48.5 mm apart — and pushes the other away. The computer measures this height as the shadow of the rectangle along the contact direction. If the laths come closer than that, a stiff spring pushes them apart. Because the push acts at the corner that touches, it also rolls the section back towards flat: the crossing rights itself.

At a crossing the contact plane contains both tangents, so the contact normal is their cross product. It is kept on its side of the crossing from one iteration to the next; that one sign is what keeps laths from passing through each other (§13):

The penalty $k_n$ (contact.strength) is a numerical stiffness, not a material one: keep it about 20–50 times the transverse stiffness of a lath over one crossing span, $48EI_x/L^3$ (65 N/mm for 60 × 30 mm at $L$ = 1 m, so $k_n \approx$ 1.3–3.2 × 10³ N/mm). Much stiffer, and 0.1 mm of geometric mismatch becomes a kilonewton. The augmented Lagrangian lets a moderate $k_n$ land on the exact gap: $\lambda$ is updated every al_every = 600 iterations by the Uzawa step of §12 — on the cm dome, strength 2 × 10³ with the multiplier gives a median penetration of 6 µm, against 0.45 mm with the default penalty alone.

  • A's support faces $+\mathbf n$, B's faces $-\mathbf n$ (_support(..., +1), _support(..., −1)); per-side width and height come from the strand's lath group.
  • Boundary knots are disabled when the weave has no overhang (a lath end resting on nothing). Locked knots (fastened protocol) use a bilateral gap.

9Friction: stuck points and Coulomb slip

While two laths stick, the two touching points are joined by an invisible stiff spring. Push sideways and the spring stretches; the friction force is the spring force. But friction can only be so strong — a fixed fraction $\mu$ of how hard the laths press together. Push harder, and the stuck points let go and slide just far enough that the spring is back at that limit. Think of a dog on an elastic leash tied to a sliding post: the post only moves when the leash is fully stretched.

Locked crossings (the fastened-structure protocol, Zero → Lock in the app) have $\mu F_n = \infty$ and a bilateral gap. And at the start the stuck points are paired at the closest points (§6): any spurious initial offset would otherwise become friction at $\mu F_n$ on the underside, whose lever $h/2\cdot F_t$ rolls sections before they can seat.

10Embedment: dents that grip

Wood is soft across the grain. Where the sharp edge of one lath presses into its neighbour, the timber crushes a little and leaves a dent, usually less than a millimetre deep. The dent is small, but it matters: the edge now sits in a groove, and to slide it has to climb out — like a chair leg stuck in a dent in a wooden floor. WeaveSim can grow these dents, make friction stronger across them and let them relieve some of the pressure between the laths. These options are off by default, until the jig tests give their parameters.

First each crossing is classified from the roll of its two sections against the contact plane (W1). The type and pressure are outputs; the type also chooses the lever radius of the scissor friction (W2) and which crossings can dent (W3). 11Dynamic relaxation: letting the weave settle

There is no formula for the shape of a whole woven shell. Instead the computer lets it move: every node gets a pretend mass and is pushed by the forces on it, like a marble rolling in a bowl. Left alone, the marble would roll back and forth for ever. The trick, due to Michael Barnes, is to watch the total speed: the moment it starts to fall, the marble has just passed the bottom of its swing — so stop it dead there and let it go again. After a few dozen such restarts it sits at the bottom. That is equilibrium.

The rod and contact forces derive from stated energies and the solver follows their negative gradient, with the omissions noted in §7 (Hermite weights) and §8 (smoothed lever); friction, the seat law and the multiplier update are non-conservative by design. Why the step is stable. For a network of springs, choosing every mass as $m_s$ times the sum of the stiffnesses at the node bounds the highest frequency by Gershgorin's theorem, $\omega_{\max}^2 \le 2/m_s$; the explicit step with $\Delta t = 1$ is stable while $\omega_{\max} \lt 2$, i.e. for $m_s \gt \tfrac12$ — Barnes' classic $m = \tfrac12\Delta t^2\sum k$ sits exactly on that line. The frames are treated alike, but bending and twist ($B_\alpha/\bar l$), the tilt of a frame against its chord ($\kappa_sGA\,l^2/l^0$), contact ($k_n+k_t$) and the seat ($k_\theta$) enter only through such equivalent stiffnesses, and the shear spring couples nodes and frames, so WeaveSim keeps a margin: $m_s = 1.5$. The price is speed. The node masses are set by the stiffest term, the axial and shear spring $(EA + \kappa_sGA)/l^0$ — 1.5 × 10⁵ N/mm per node for 60 × 30 mm laths in 250 mm segments, 95 % of it axial — while the bending modes that shape a shell have stiffnesses of the order of 50 N/mm, so they converge slowly; a 10⁴ N/mm contact penalty adds another 10–15 % to the nodes next to a crossing.

How far from buckling: the stability check

A relaxed state can be an equilibrium and still be about to give way — a dome before it snaps through, a lath before it rolls over. The Stability group of the Monitor, beside the 3-D view (Check now, or after every run), measures how stiff the softest way of deforming the whole model is: as a load approaches buckling, that stiffness falls towards zero; below zero the state is unstable.

$\mathbf g$ are the solver's generalised forces (rod, contact with its stick–slip return mapping and multipliers frozen at the current state, loads), $\mathbf q$ the free degrees of freedom — the free node translations and the three body-axis rotations of every segment frame — and $\mathbf M$ the relaxation masses and inertias, so $\lambda$ is the squared frequency of the relaxation dynamics. $\mathbf K$ is assembled from central differences of $\mathbf g$ with a distance-2 colouring of the stencil (under a hundred force evaluations for a single lath, about 1100 for the 80-face dome), $\mathbf A$ is factorised as $\mathbf L\mathbf D\mathbf L^{\mathsf T}$ on a nested-dissection ordering, the number of negative pivots is the number of unstable modes (Sylvester's law of inertia) and inverse iteration gives $\lambda_0$. Checks (web/tests/stability_check.mjs): an Euler column gives $\lambda_0/\lambda_0(P{=}0)$ = 0.49 / 0.086 / −0.12 at $P/P_E$ = 0.5 / 0.9 / 1.1, close to the line $1 - P/P_{cr}$ with the Haringx load $P_{cr} = 0.986\,P_E$ of the shear-flexible column (§5), and one unstable mode above it; the lateral–torsional strip of §4 crosses zero at 21.0 N against 21.1 N (the earlier shear-free rod, without the roll moment, never did). On the 80-face dome (5940 degrees of freedom) it takes a few seconds.

A frame that will not sit still

The push between two laths acts on the corner that touches. Within a sliver of a degree the push jumps from one corner to the other, so a section that overshoots gets kicked back just as hard — and if each step is bigger than that sliver, it overshoots again, for ever.

The smoothed support point makes the lever a bang-bang torque $\pm F_n w/2$ outside the $\pm\varepsilon$ window. The frame's roll inertia, about its Z axis, is set from the window's stiffness, $(F_n + k_nh/4)(w/2)/\varepsilon$; once $F_n \gg k_nh/4$, as under the runaway multiplier of the thin laths, the saturated torque accelerates the frame by up to $\varepsilon/m_s\approx 1.15°$ per iteration² — it crosses most of the 3.4° window in each step and oscillates by ±3–5° indefinitely, an undamped bang-bang oscillator that the global kinetic damping does not see. Worse, the support height $D$ is convex in the roll, so a chattering section looks thicker on average and biases the penetration the multiplier feeds on.

13Why laths don’t pass through each other

In a computer nothing physically stops one lath from moving straight through another — only the forces we program. If a lath moves too far in one step, or if the program forgets which lath is on top, it can end up on the wrong side, and then the same forces happily keep it there. Characters in video games fall through walls the same way. WeaveSim guards against it at four levels.

  1. Over and under is topology, not geometry. Each crossing knows from the weave pattern (§2) which lath is on top, the initial geometry is offset accordingly, and the contact normal keeps its side from iteration to iteration (eq. ). Even if two laths overlap for a moment, $g$ goes negative, $p = D - g$ grows beyond $D$ and the force pushes them back the right way: there is no stable passed-through state.
  2. Every crossing keeps its own partner. Bookmarks are tracked per crossing, not found by a global closest-point search, so a double crossing or a self-intersection cannot swap partners, and the bookmarks along a lath keep their order (§6).
  3. No step is big enough to jump. Masses from stiffness keep every translation small compared with the stiffest spring; the slide cap limits a bookmark to 15 mm per iteration; the roll-rate limit keeps sections from spinning past a corner in one step (§12). This is the same idea as the per-vertex displacement bounds of the Offset Geometric Contact method (Chen et al. 2025), which the Kangaroo version borrowed in February.
  4. No force grows without limit. A runaway force is the one thing that can overcome all of the above, by rolling a section past its diagonal. For a $w\times h$ section the support height peaks at $\varphi^* = \arctan(w/h)$ — 63.4° for 60 × 30, 68.2° for 20 × 8 mm; beyond it the lever of §8 changes sign and drives the section onto its next face. In the October incident the runaway multiplier rolled the soft 2 × 0.8 cm laths past 68°, the support function jumped from face to edge ($D$ from 0.8 to 1.4 cm), the penetration jumped, the multiplier ratcheted faster, and the laths went through each other (gap −0.5 cm) and flipped. The multiplier safeguards closed this path.

Earlier forms of the same problem, all in the Kangaroo version: Kangaroo merges particles that coincide, which locked laths together at their crossings (February); the lever torque flipped sign at face-to-face crossings and the closest segment pair alternated at knots, which made sections jitter (fixed in PolylineContact v3, September).

Known limits

  • The rod as implemented (§4): section properties are constant along each segment; shear uses the coefficient $\kappa_s = 5/6$ of a solid rectangle; torsion is St Venant's ($GJ$), without warping restraint; and each frame's rotational inertia is lumped and fictitious, so it shapes only the pseudo-dynamics of the relaxation (§11), not the equilibria.
  • The path between equilibria is pseudo-time. The equilibria are genuine, but friction is path-dependent, so the order of loading in a simulation is a modelling choice.
  • Stick–slip switching is non-smooth: residuals plateau, typically at 10⁻² to 10⁻¹ (§11).
  • Contact forces go back to the nodes with the linear weights $(1-t,\ t)$, an approximation of the exact transpose for the Hermite curve (§7).
  • Sections are rigid; elastic embedment is ignored on purpose (it is invisible), plastic seats are optional and their parameters are placeholders until the jig tests (§10).
  • Material strength enters the solver only through $f_{c,90}$: the cap on the multiplier (§12) and the dents of the seat law (§10). Stress and minimum-spacing checks are outputs, not constraints (§2).
  • Creep is a sequence of equilibria through the activation, not a time integration (§4).
  • Calibration on one topology may not transfer to another: a dome pre-compresses its crossings, a saddle can starve them of normal force, so the same $\mu$ does different work. This transfer is itself a research question of the project.

15How the model grew

  1. February 2026, in Grasshopper

    A specification for a reciprocal timber weave in Kangaroo: a global parameter $u$ that slides across segments, a section-aware gap, stick–slip friction. Three community goals as ingredients — LineAt2LineAt (Piker, reciprocal support), swivel.cs (plane alignment) and friction.cs (stuck targets). A two-beam test slides; Kangaroo's particle merging is found to lock crossings.

  2. Late August

    A first standalone Python solver (projective dynamics, Kangaroo's weighted average) and the naming question for Prof. Williams: “a discrete Cosserat-type rod, implemented as a projection”. A dimensional inconsistency between translation and rotation weights in the swivel goal is identified as the likely cause of an $I_x/I_y$ calibration anomaly.

  3. Early September

    WeaveInit v3 (BFS over/under parity, frames X width / Y normal / Z tangent, pre-offset knots) and PolylineContact v2: windowed closest point, rectangle support function, persistent side normal, lever torque, FloorFriction stick–slip, Lock / Unlock. The design notes add per-pair search areas, ordered contacts against pass-through, and contact forces shared to the neighbouring knots.

  4. 28 September

    ActiveBeam: SOFiSTiK-style active bending with a straight rest state, true-angle rotation measures. PolylineContact v3: lever torque sign, segment pair alternation, friction per lath, friction delay during the prestress ramp.

  5. 29 September – 1 October

    WeaveSim, the standalone program: dynamic relaxation with fictitious masses and Barnes kinetic damping, the discrete elastic rod, the Hermite contact centreline, physical units. The Solver Notes describe it.

  6. 4 October

    Crossing mechanics from the review: contact types, scissor friction, the directional seat law; the augmented-Lagrangian gap.

  7. 14–15 October

    WeaveSim Web: the CPU f64 reference and a batched WebGPU solver in 64-bit fixed point, the calibration sandbox, the Zero → Lock protocol, public on GitHub Pages.

  8. 17 October

    Safeguarded multipliers and the roll-rate limit, after laths flipped and passed through each other under extreme load. This page.

  9. 20 October

    After an external code review and discussions at the school: the Cosserat rod (shear, roll moment, lateral–torsional buckling), the stability check, and crossing histories — the friction-coefficient evolution and other quantities at chosen crossings. Later the same day the Cosserat rod became the only rod of both web solvers (the Python program keeps the shear-free rod), and the charts moved into the Monitor beside the 3-D view; the playback of the solve history was dropped, as it cost too much speed and memory.

Words

Active bending
Forming a structure by bending elastic members; the bending stress it leaves behind is the prestress.
Augmented Lagrangian
Enforcing a constraint exactly with a moderate spring, by adding a force that is learned step by step until the gap is right.
Cosserat rod
A line that carries a rotating frame, free to tip against the line as well as to turn with it: the general model of a slender elastic body, and WeaveSim's model of a lath.
Coulomb friction
Friction proportional to how hard two surfaces press together, independent of the contact area.
Dynamic relaxation
Finding the equilibrium of a structure by letting it move with pretend masses and taking the motion out.
Embedment
Permanent local crushing where one piece of timber presses into another across its grain.
Kagome
The triaxial weave of hexagons and triangles, named after a traditional Japanese basket pattern.
Kinetic damping
Stopping all motion each time the total kinetic energy passes a peak.
Kirchhoff rod
A Cosserat rod that cannot shear: its sections stay square to the centreline — the limit of infinite shear stiffness.
Lateral–torsional buckling
A lath bent about its strong axis that gives way by bending sideways and rolling at the same time.
Penalty
A stiff spring that enforces a constraint approximately; the stiffer, the more exact and the slower.
Return mapping
After a trial step that breaks the friction limit, pulling the state back exactly onto the limit.
Second-order theory
Structural analysis that writes equilibrium on the deformed shape, so axial forces change bending stiffness.
Support function
The half-thickness of a shape measured in a given direction.
